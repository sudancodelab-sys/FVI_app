The Night the River Rose

A sudden flood cuts off the village where Sara's team built its solar pump. Read the story, then read it from the point of view of an engineer on her team.

Give emphasis with inversionTell a dramatic storyDescribe how people work together in a crisis

1The story

Sara had visited the village near Kassala many times since her team built the solar pump, but she had never seen the sky like this. Never had the old men of the village seen so much rain in one afternoon. By evening, the Gash River, which is dry for most of the year, had become a brown, roaring wall of water.

Not only did the river rise faster than anyone expected, but the road to Kassala was also cut off within an hour. Fifty families lived on the low side of the village, close to the water. Sara, Tarig, and Mona ran from house to house, telling people to move to the school, which stood on higher ground.

Hardly had the last family reached the school when the water poured into the empty streets behind them. Two hundred people were crowded into four classrooms, most of them wet and frightened. Then a woman ran to Sara. "My father needs his heart medicine," she said, "and it's in our house." The house was already under water.

Sara called the one person she knew who could get medicine through a flood. Omar's company had spent a year testing drones that carry medicine to villages. "Give me two hours," he said. "Keep your phone on."

Only then did Sara realize how tired she was, but there was no time to rest. Mona kept the children calm with games and stories. Tarig connected the batteries from the pump to the school, so the classrooms had light all night. The women cooked rice in the yard, and the men carried clean water from the pump's tank. Everyone had a job. "Many hands make light work," said one of the old men.

At two in the morning, they heard a soft buzzing above the school. A small drone came down out of the darkness and landed in the yard with a box. Inside were the medicine, some bandages, and a note. From Omar's Technology. With an H this time. Sara laughed for the first time that night.

By the next afternoon, the water had gone down, and a Red Crescent truck arrived. Never before had the village felt so close. "You were a ray of hope for us," the woman told Sara. Sara shook her head and pointed at the people in the yard. "No," she said. "We all were."

3Point of view Now Mona tells it.

MO

I have worked on many projects, but never have I been as frightened as I was that night. The rain didn't stop for six hours, and the river rose faster than any of us expected.

My job was the children. More than eighty of them were in one classroom, terrified of the noise, so we played games and sang until they fell asleep. Not once did they stop asking about their homes.

Little did we know that Omar was already on his way. When his drone landed in the yard, the children who were still awake started cheering, and the ones who were asleep woke up.

Only later did I understand what we had done. Nobody had been hurt, and every family had a dry place to sleep.

4What did you learn?

You learned inversion for emphasis.

After a negative or limiting word at the start of a sentence, the auxiliary verb comes before the subject, as in a question. It sounds formal and dramatic.

Normal word orderWith inversion
The village had never seen such rain.Never had the village seen such rain.
The river rose, and the road closed too.Not only did the river rise, but…
As soon as they arrived, the water came.Hardly had they arrived when it came.
She realized it only then.Only then did she realize it.
We didn't know he was coming.Little did we know he was coming.
Word + auxiliary + subjectNever have I seen… Rarely do we hear… Not once did they stop…
Past simple: add didThe river rose. Not only did the river rise…
Use it carefullyIt is dramatic. In everyday speech, normal word order is better.
QuestionsWhat have you never seen in your life?Never have I seen snow.
